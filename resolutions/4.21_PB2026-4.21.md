Cutting the Red Tape: Unleashing Europe's Economic Engine Considering that:

* The International Monetary Fund (IMF) predicts an economic growth of only 1

  % for the Eurozone in 2024, while it predicts 2,7 % for the United States.

* In fact, low economic growth rates in the EU compared to the US can be observed for several years now, particularly since the coronavirus crisis.

* The lack of growth threatens our European Way of Life as our social security systems, our environmental goals and our resilience against dictatorships depend on a strong economy.

* Historically, the EU has been a promoter of economic freedom, market liberalisation and free trade and thus, it contributed large parts to the economic growth witnessed in past decades.

* However, under the leadership of Ursula von der Leyen the European Commission has done the opposite: a record number of bureaucratic laws

have been passed that strangle the economy, especially small and medium-sized enterprises (SME).

* Another example is the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) which was passed with good intentions in order to protect patients from unsafe medical devices; however, the new certification mechanism for medical devices of the MDR prolonged certification and leads to costs which are up to 150 times higher than in the US threatening a shortage of medical devices for little children which are only produced in small numbers.

* In addition, the EU has lost its avant garde position with regard to free trade.

* While it is necessary to reconsider and at times cut trade relations with autocratic regimes, to pursue trade with democratic states should be top priority.

* The Commission for 2019-2024 has provisionally applied the EU-New Zealand Trade Agreement, and the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement. Additionally, other agreements that have already entered into force include the EU-Japan Economic Partnership Agreement, the EU-Singapore Free Trade Agreement, and the EU-Vietnam Free Trade Agreement.

Acknowledging that:

* While the economic success of a single Member State and Europe as a whole does not only depend on EU policy but is also in the responsibility of each and every Member States, EU policy plays a decisive role in the economic success of the EU and its Member States.

Believing that:

* The new European Commission must radically cut bureaucracy, finalise the internal market and pursue free trade agreements with democracies around the globe in order to make Europe more competitive and increase economic growth.

LYMEC calls for the reduction of bureaucracy and other regulatory burdens, in concrete for:

* The expansion of the definition of small and medium-sized enterprises (SME) to enterprises with a market capitalisation larger than 50 Mio. Euros and up to  
  1.250 employees.

* Support for Small Businesses: Simplified trade regulations that enable small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to access global markets, fostering innovation and competition.

* The independent review of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Digital Markets Act (DMA), the Digital Services Act (DSA) and the AI Act in terms of practicality, legal certainty and their effects on innovation, SME and national trade barriers.

* Digital Trade and Privacy Protection: A commitment to promote cross-border digital trade with harmonised data protection standards, balancing privacy rights and economic openness.

* The implementation of a “fair use doctrine” for AI training data as in theUnited States.

* The restriction of the Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) Directive to projects with cross-border impact.

* A reform of the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) that introduces an easy recertification mechanism for medical products that were already on the market prior to the MDRs’ enter into force, a low-cost certification mechanism for medical products designed for a small group of patients, especially children and people with rare diseases, and an overall reform of the MDR to reduce costs and time needed for the certification.

LYMEC calls for the further integration of the internal market, in concrete for:

* The concentration of the registration of products on an EU level or the assurance of mutual recognition between Member States.

* Preferring full harmonisation in contrast to minimum standards and the assurance of a uniform interpretation of the law (for example through enhanced administrative cooperation or the establishment of EU agencies) in order to remove trade barriers.

* The removal of protectionist national regulations on services that in practice exclude enterprises and citizens from other Member States (e.g. the German “Meisterzwang” or the prohibition on third-party ownership for law firms and pharmacies).

* The establishment of a Common European Sales Law which can be voluntarily chosen (opt-in) by contracting parties to ease cross border sales.

* The finalisation of the capital markets union and the banking union, if necessary through the procedure of enhanced cooperation.

* Consumer Safety Standards: Mutual recognition of public health and safety standards to protect consumers without imposing unnecessary trade barriers.

LYMEC calls for the expansion of free trade with democracies, in concrete for:

* The Member States to immediately ratify the EU-Canada Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), and the EU-Chile

Advanced Framework Agreement.

* The Member States to modify the treaties in order to enable the EU to ratify comprehensive trade agreements which include provisions on portfolio investments and investment protection with a qualified majority in the Council of the EU.

* The European Commission to swiftly conclude negotiations on trade and investment agreements with Australia, the ASEAN states, India and Taiwan.
