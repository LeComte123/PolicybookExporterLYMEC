Recognising that:

- Bullfighting has existed as a cultural tradition in several European countries, including Spain, Portugal, and southern France, for centuries
- Traditions rooted in economic and cultural practice have already been overridden by the European Union on animal-welfare grounds.
- The European Union has previously exempted bullfighting from animal welfare legislation under the cultural heritage clause, allowing member states to maintain the practice.
- Council Regulation (EC) 1099/2009 on the protection of animals at the time of killing excludes animals killed during cultural or sporting events from its scope, meaning bulls killed in the ring are not subject to the welfare standards applied to animals slaughtered for food.
- Bullfighting is a contest involving a human voluntarily immobilising, injuring and/or killing a bull. This should not be confused with other contests involving other animals, such as Swiss cow fighting, which do not involve direct human intervention and are not detrimental to animal welfare.

Noting that:

- Bullfighting causes severe and deliberate physical and psychological suffering to bulls, in direct contradiction with the principle of animal sentience recognised in Article 13 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU).
- Bullfighting also carries serious risks to human life: recorded data show that 134 people have been killed by bulls in Spain over the past century, with many more suffering serious injuries. These events require publicly funded emergency and security services, reinforcing the industry's public subsidy.
- Public support for bullfighting is in sharp decline across Europe, including in Spain, where a majority of the population, and particularly young people, oppose the practice.
- The framing of bullfighting as "cultural heritage" sets a dangerous precedent by allowing cultural tradition to override fundamental ethical and animal welfare standards.
- There are regions, such as Catalonia and the Canary Islands, where bullfighting is already mostly abolished.
- Bull-breeding farms continue to receive Common Agricultural Policy direct payments regardless of whether the animals are destined for the bullring, meaning EU taxpayers indirectly subsidise the practice.
- Spanish regulation (Real Decreto 1086/2020) classifies the killing of bulls in bullfights as "emergency slaughter" under Regulation (EC) 853/2004, a classification that raises food safety concerns where such meat enters the human food chain.

Believing that:

- The deliberate killing and torture of animals for entertainment is fundamentally incompatible with a humane society, regardless of cultural or historical tradition.
- No tradition should be permitted to override the Union's commitment to animal sentience and welfare.
- In Spain, France and Portugal, 77% of the population considers bullfighting to cause unacceptable animal suffering, and 58% want it banned, numbers that are difficult to reconcile with continued public support for the tradition.
- A just transition must be offered to workers and breeders currently dependent on the bullfighting industry, to ensure that ending the practice does not come at the expense of rural livelihoods.

LYMEC calls for:

- All member states to ban bullfighting, and calls on the Commission to support and coordinate such efforts.
- The revision of Article 13 TFEU to remove or narrow the cultural exemption insofar as it permits practices causing deliberate and severe animal suffering
- The European Commission to revise Regulation (EC) 1099/2009 to remove the exemption for cultural and sporting events, ensuring bulls killed in bullfights are subject to the same welfare protections as other slaughtered animals
- An immediate end to all direct and indirect public subsidies at local, regional, and national level, directed towards the breeding, training, or staging of bullfighting.
- The European Commission to investigate the classification of bullring killings as "emergency slaughter" under Regulation (EC) 853/2004, given its implications for food safety.
- The Commission and Member States to establish transition support schemes for workers and breeders affected by the phase-out of the bullfighting industry. The reallocation of Common Agricultural Policy funds currently supporting bull-breeding towards the conservation of rural ecosystems, ensuring that the animals currently bred for the bullring can be maintained in natural sanctuaries rather than being subjected to deliberate suffering.
