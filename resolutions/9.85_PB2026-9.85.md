**Passed Spring 2026**

Submitted by: Junge liberale NEOS (Austria)

Co-signed by: Jóvenes de Ciudadanos (Spain), Jongliberalen (Belgium), Jungfreisinnige Schweiz (Switzerland), Young Green Liberals (Switzerland), ZeMolodizhka (Ukraine), Mladi hrvatski liberali (Croatia), Venstres Ungdom (Denmark), Momentum TizenX (Hungary), PSG Mladi (Serbia), Junge Liberale \- JuLis (Germany), JOVD (The Netherlands), Youth forum of Naša stranka (Bosnia and Herzegovina), Liberale Hochschulgruppen (Germany), Jonge Democraten (The Netherlands)

Noting that:

* Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 has fundamentally altered the European security environment and demonstrated the continued relevance of nuclear deterrence in global geopolitics.  
* The Russian Federation continues to maintain and modernise one of the world’s largest nuclear arsenals while repeatedly using nuclear threats as a tool of coercion against European democracies.  
* The security architecture of Europe has long relied on the nuclear umbrella of the United States through NATO, which has been essential in maintaining peace and deterrence on the continent.  
* Growing geopolitical instability and increasing uncertainty regarding the long-term reliability of U.S. security guarantees highlight the need for Europe to strengthen its strategic autonomy within the transatlantic alliance.  
* That France and the United Kingdom, the only two European nuclear powers, have recognized a European dimension to their deterrence strategies in the Northwood declaration.  
* France is currently the only nuclear-armed member of the European Union and has indicated in its forward deterrence doctrine its willingness to open cooperation and coordination on nuclear deterrence with European partners.  
* European defence cooperation has intensified in recent years, including initiatives aimed at strengthening joint defence capabilities and strategic coordination within the European Union.  
* The UK and several member states have already signaled their interest in participating in France’s forward deterrence.

Acknowledging that:

* Nuclear weapons represent a grave humanitarian risk and the long-term objective of liberal security policy must remain global nuclear disarmament.  
* However, as long as authoritarian powers such as Russia and People's Republic of China maintain nuclear arsenals and use nuclear threats to destabilise democratic societies, credible deterrence remains a necessary component of European security.

Believing that:

* The protection of liberal democracy, human rights, and European sovereignty requires credible and effective deterrence against nuclear coercion and aggression.  
* That while short of being a true nuclear umbrella, France’s forward deterrence is an important step towards EU strategic autonomy aggression.  
* The European Union with its own foreign and security policy needs an European armed force.  
* A European framework for nuclear deterrence would reduce strategic dependency, increase collective resilience, and ensure that decisions affecting European security are made with democratic accountability.  
* Shared responsibility among European states would provide greater stability and reliability than reliance on a single national nuclear deterrent.  
* Democratic oversight, transparency, and civilian control are essential elements of any European security architecture.

LYMEC calls for:

* The governments of European Union Member States and democratic partners on the continent to begin structured discussions on the development of a European nuclear deterrence framework within the broader context of European defence integration, with the goal being joint European Armed Forces.  
* The European Union and its Member States to invest in nuclear research and technological development, including civilian nuclear energy and relevant dual-use capabilities, in order to reinforce Europe’s strategic resilience, maintain technological expertise, and support a credible and autonomous security architecture.  
* The command structure must be clearly defined and democratically legitimate. It must moreover be pan-European in nature and must not be under the control of individual Member States. However, until this can be achieved, individual Member States should also be able to proceed in accordance with Article 46 of the EU Treaty and bring French nuclear weapons under joint controlThis should, of course, only take place with the consent of the countries that provide the nuclear weapons \- nuclear weapons from France, for example, should not be able to be placed under joint control against their will by a qualified majority.  
* France to initiate consultations with European partners on extending its nuclear deterrence as a temporary European nuclear umbrella while a broader European framework is developed.  
* The European Union and willing Member and Partner States to explore mechanisms for shared financial, strategic, and operational responsibility for nuclear deterrence in Europe.  
* The development, in the long term, of a European defence structure in which nuclear deterrence responsibilities are embedded within a future European defence union or European army under democratic European command and oversight. This shall also include the development of a strategic submarine fleet to ensure a second-strike capability.  
* The European Parliament to be involved in shaping democratic accountability mechanisms for European-level security and deterrence policies.  
* The European Union and its Member States to continue pursuing international nuclear arms control and disarmament efforts while ensuring that Europe maintains a credible deterrent as long as nuclear threats persist.
