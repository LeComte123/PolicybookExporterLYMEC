This motion replaces: 6.03 The future is Nuclear

Considering that:

- Electricity demand in Europe will rise significantly as transport, heating and industry are electrified, and a competitive economy depends on affordable electricity that is available at every hour of the year;
- Nuclear energy generates almost a quarter of the electricity produced in the European Union with very low lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions;
- Several Member States that decided to phase out nuclear energy are now reversing or reconsidering these decisions, while early reactor closures have in some cases prolonged the use of fossil fuels for electricity generation;
- Nineteen reactors of Soviet design in five Member States were built to run on Russian fuel, alternative supply for these reactors is still being established, and the European Union continues to import nuclear fuel and enrichment services from the Russian Federation;
- Only a few Member States have made concrete progress towards deep geological repositories for spent nuclear fuel, and the rules on operator liability for nuclear damage and on the financing of decommissioning differ widely between Member States;
- Under the Treaty establishing the European Atomic Energy Community, the European Parliament is in most cases only consulted, which leaves central decisions on nuclear safety, research and supply without democratic co-decision;
- The United Kingdom and the United States have decided to regulate fusion energy outside their licensing regimes for nuclear fission, recognising its fundamentally different risk profile.

Noting that:

- The Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union guarantees each Member State the right to determine the structure of its energy supply;
- Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/1214 recognises nuclear energy as sustainable under conditions and Regulation (EU) 2024/1747 opens two-sided contracts for difference to it, while the InvestEU Programme still excludes nuclear power plants from support;
- Large bidding zones and uniform network charges do not reflect physical grid bottlenecks, which leads to costly redispatch, curtailment of renewable generation and higher total system costs for consumers;
- The European Commission has launched the European Industrial Alliance on Small Modular Reactors and presented a strategy to deploy small modular reactors in Europe by the early 2030s.

Believing that:

- Climate protection is achieved most efficiently through a strong European Union Emissions Trading System that sets one price on carbon, while governments neither ban nor subsidise specific technologies, leaving the choice, including the role of nuclear energy, to consumers, investors and innovators on the basis of real costs and prices;
- Nuclear safety is not negotiable and must be guaranteed by independent regulators on the basis of science, not by political deadlines;
- Energy supply that depends on autocratic regimes is a security risk and finances aggression against Europe;
- Fusion energy can provide clean and abundant energy, and Europe should lead its development instead of regulating it away to other continents.

LYMEC calls for:

1\. A competitive and technology-neutral electricity market

- The retention of the merit order principle as the basis of European wholesale electricity markets;
- Electricity prices that reflect the real costs of generation, transmission and load at each place and time, through a reconfiguration of bidding zones along physical grid constraints and, in the long term, locational pricing;
- A reform of network charges towards time-variable and location-based tariffs, so that regions with high generation benefit from lower prices and total system costs fall for all users;
- The consistent phase-out of state subsidies for all mature generation technologies, including fixed feed-in tariffs, and no new long-term revenue guarantees such as two-sided contracts for difference for any generation technology;
- The removal of remaining regulatory barriers for energy storage, in particular the double charging of network fees and levies on stored electricity, and faster grid connection for projects that combine generation and storage;
- Faster grid expansion through simplified and shorter permitting procedures and the most cost-efficient construction of new power lines;
- A significantly faster rollout of smart metering systems and dynamic electricity tariffs, enabling digital grid management and the coupling of the electricity, heating and mobility sectors;
- The European Investment Bank and all European Union funding instruments, including the InvestEU Programme, to treat all low-carbon technologies equally, neither privileging nor excluding nuclear energy.

2\. The full-cost principle for nuclear energy

- Operators of nuclear power plants to bear the cost of their activity, including decommissioning, waste disposal and liability for nuclear damage;
- Mandatory decommissioning and waste disposal funds for all operators, separated from company assets, supervised by independent authorities and sized to cover the expected costs;
- Harmonised European rules obliging operators to cover their liability for nuclear damage through insurance, financial security or pooled operator schemes, with minimum coverage based on realistic accident costs;
- Member States to permit the construction of new nuclear power plants wherever operators bear these costs.

3\. Ending political bans and modernising nuclear law

- Member States to repeal legal bans on building, operating, extending and researching nuclear technologies, to modernise their nuclear legislation, and to grant independent regulators the sole authority to determine whether a nuclear installation is safe to operate, without limiting the right of operators to close a plant for economic reasons or the right of Member States to determine their energy mix through democratic decisions;
- Reactors that fail to meet the requirements of Council Directive 2009/71/Euratom establishing a Community framework for the nuclear safety of nuclear installations, as verified by independent European peer review, to be shut down;
- Member States to allow the conversion of shut-down reactors into research facilities.

4\. A European framework for licensing, innovation and safety

- The harmonisation of national rules for the operation, lifetime and construction of nuclear installations within the single European energy market;
- The mutual recognition of reactor design approvals between national nuclear regulators, so that a design approved in one Member State does not require a complete new assessment in another;
- Cross-border regulatory sandboxes for small modular reactors and fourth-generation designs such as molten salt reactors, fast breeder reactors and travelling wave reactors;
- The freedom of companies to invest in the research, development and deployment of these technologies in every Member State;
- The regular revision of risk assessments for all nuclear installations against natural disasters, terrorist attacks, cyber attacks and drone incursions, and the protection of nuclear installations as critical infrastructure.

5\. Fuel sovereignty

- A ban on new contracts with entities from the Russian Federation for uranium, conversion, enrichment and nuclear fuel, and a legally binding phase-out of existing supplies by 2030 at the latest;
- The inclusion of Rosatom and its subsidiaries in European Union sanctions;
- The accelerated licensing of alternative fuel for reactors of Soviet design and the expansion of conversion and enrichment capacity by European and allied suppliers.

6\. Solving the waste question through research and European cooperation

- Increased research funding for technologies that reduce the volume, radiotoxicity and lifetime of radioactive waste, in particular reprocessing and transmutation, including through the Euratom Research and Training Programme;
- Member States to lift national bans on the reprocessing of spent fuel, under strict Euratom and International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards that prevent any military use of the resulting plutonium;
- Deep geological repositories to be designed so that waste remains retrievable for future recycling technologies;
- The establishment of a European expert body to identify geologically suitable sites for shared multinational repositories in Member States willing to host them;
- A financing model for shared repositories in which host countries are compensated, operators pay for the waste they produce and Member States cover legacy waste in proportion to their share of nuclear generation.

7\. Fusion energy

- A European legal framework for fusion energy outside the law on nuclear fission, proportionate to its risk profile and open to innovation;
- The European Union to become the global frontrunner in fusion energy by supporting basic research, demonstration plants and lighthouse projects, including ITER and its successor DEMO, as well as public-private partnerships with fusion start-ups;
- Research budgets for fusion and other breakthrough energy technologies that accept failures as a normal part of research.

8\. Democratic control over European nuclear policy

- A reform of the Treaty establishing the European Atomic Energy Community that grants the European Parliament full co-decision rights on all nuclear legislation.

9\. International research and material partnerships

- Deepened cooperation with trusted democratic partners on nuclear research, reactor design, and the safe handling of nuclear materials, building on existing frameworks such as Euratom cooperation agreements;
- Joint investment with these partners in diversifying the supply of uranium, conversion, and enrichment services away from dependence on the Russian Federation, including support for mining and enrichment capacity in allied and partner countries;
- Coordinated export-control and non-proliferation standards among these partners, ensuring that expanded research and material cooperation strengthens the international non-proliferation regime rather than weakening it.
