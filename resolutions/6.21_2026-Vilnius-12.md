Noting that:

- Europe must rapidly decarbonise its economy while maintaining affordable and reliable electricity supplies and safeguarding its industrial competitiveness;
- the electrification of transport, heating and industrial processes will increase the importance of a reliable, affordable and low-carbon electricity system, while tax, levy and tariff structures in some EU Member States continue to disadvantage electricity relative to the direct use of fossil fuels;
- EU Member States differ considerably in geography, natural resources, existing infrastructure and public preferences and therefore rely on different electricity mixes, while Article 194 TFEU preserves their right to determine their choice between different energy sources;
- renewable energy sources such as wind, solar and hydropower are essential to European decarbonisation, while growing shares of variable generation increase the importance of grids, storage, demand response, interconnection and other sources of flexibility;
- nuclear energy provides a substantial share of Europe’s low-carbon electricity and is used by several EU Member States for decarbonisation and security of supply, while requiring the highest standards of safety, security, radiation protection, decommissioning and radioactive-waste management;
- Europe remains dependent on external suppliers for parts of several critical electricity-sector supply chains, including parts of the nuclear fuel cycle;
- a more interconnected European electricity system allows countries to make better use of different generation profiles, reduce the effects of local shortages and surpluses and increase overall security of supply;
- the deployment of new generation, storage and flexible demand is increasingly constrained by lengthy permitting procedures, limited grid-connection capacity and inefficiently managed connection queues;
- electricity generation and network infrastructure require substantial long-term investment, while fragmented national licensing requirements and regulatory practices can increase costs, duplicate assessments and impede economies of scale within the European Single Market.

Acknowledging that:

- no electricity technology is without costs, risks, environmental impacts or infrastructure requirements, and technology neutrality therefore requires comparable risks, services and externalities to be assessed through transparent, evidence-based and proportionate criteria rather than all technologies being regulated identically;
- the costs and benefits of electricity technologies cannot be meaningfully assessed solely through generation costs but must also account for relevant system effects, including balancing, grids, storage, firm capacity, security of supply, decommissioning, waste management and environmental externalities;
- public intervention can be justified where markets cannot adequately provide infrastructure, innovation, security of supply or other public goods, but poorly designed subsidies, price controls and other interventions can distort competition and investment; long-term investment therefore requires regulatory frameworks that are predictable and non-retroactive while remaining open to transparent and proportionate future reform;
- energy-only markets and functioning price signals should remain the starting point for coordinating investment, production and consumption, while additional capacity mechanisms may be justified where demonstrable adequacy concerns remain after barriers to interconnection, storage, demand response and market participation have been addressed;
- scarce grid capacity should be allocated transparently and efficiently rather than being indefinitely occupied by inactive or speculative projects, while flexible or conditional connection agreements can in some cases enable earlier access without immediate full network reinforcement;
- the transition away from fossil-fuel electricity generation must be sufficiently rapid to meet Europe’s climate objectives while avoiding new long-term dependencies on infrastructure that risks becoming economically or environmentally unsustainable;
- greater European regulatory cooperation and technical standardisation can reduce unnecessary duplication and enable economies of scale without weakening the independence or ultimate responsibility of competent national safety regulators.

Believing that:

- European electricity policy should pursue climate neutrality, security of supply and affordability by judging technologies according to measurable outcomes—including lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions, safety, environmental impacts, system value and economic efficiency—rather than prescribing a politically preferred technological pathway;
- Europe should neither politically mandate nuclear energy nor exclude it from a future climate-neutral electricity system;
- competition, carbon pricing and functioning markets are generally better suited to discovering cost-effective solutions than permanent technology-specific subsidies or political micromanagement;
- electricity prices provide essential signals of scarcity, abundance, congestion and investment needs; affordability concerns and energy poverty should therefore primarily be addressed through targeted support rather than broad price caps or permanent administrative price-setting that suppress these signals;
- taxation, network charges and regulation should, as far as possible, reflect measurable costs and externalities rather than favouring or penalising electricity technologies merely according to their technological category;
- where public financial support or procurement is necessary, authorities should define the outcome or service required and allow competing technologies to determine how it can be delivered most efficiently, using transparent, proportionate, time-limited and competitive mechanisms wherever possible;
- competitive neutrality requires publicly and privately owned electricity companies to compete under equivalent market, regulatory and State aid conditions, while consumers should be able to participate actively through dynamic prices, flexibility, aggregators and competing contractual models;
- deeper European integration should reduce unnecessary national fragmentation while preserving proportionate regulation and allowing scientific evidence, technological progress and changing economic conditions to alter Europe’s electricity choices over time.

LYMEC calls for:

I. Technology-neutral climate neutrality

- European climate and electricity policy to pursue rapid and sustained reductions in greenhouse gas emissions without prescribing that climate neutrality must be achieved through a 100% renewable, nuclear-free or otherwise predetermined technological pathway;
- EU Member States to remain free to determine their own low-carbon electricity mix, including whether to use nuclear energy, while complying with common European climate, environmental, competition and safety standards;
- the rapid deployment of low-carbon generation and supporting infrastructure, including renewable generation, grids, storage and flexibility, while allowing nuclear energy to remain part of the long-term electricity mix in countries that choose to use it;
- EU Member States choosing to use nuclear energy to be free, subject to independent safety assessment and national policy choices, to construct new capacity, extend the operation of existing reactors and assess the technically and economically viable recommissioning of recently closed capacity; provided that such decisions are based on transparent lifecycle cost assessments, credible and fully financed plans for decommissioning and radioactive-waste management, adequate operator liability for nuclear accidents, and the internalisation of relevant environmental and safety risks.
- regulatory, investment, sustainable-finance and State aid frameworks for low-carbon electricity to apply transparent and scientifically grounded criteria to technologies providing comparable climate and system benefits, while public procurement should specify measurable outcomes such as emissions intensity, reliability and system services rather than prescribe the technology used to deliver them;
- Member States to facilitate the repowering and expansion of existing low-carbon generation sites where existing grid connections, infrastructure and land use make this more efficient than comparable greenfield development, subject to the same substantive environmental and safety requirements;
- strong European research and innovation across renewable generation, storage, advanced grids, advanced nuclear technologies, Small Modular Reactors and nuclear fusion, with public research funding focused particularly on fundamental research, demonstration, first-of-a-kind technologies and identified market failures while commercially mature technologies increasingly rely on private capital;
- the progressive replacement of unabated fossil-fuel electricity generation by low-carbon alternatives while avoiding unnecessary long-lived fossil generation assets and carbon lock-in.
- the phase-out of fossil fuels across the economy (including in transport, heating, and industry) to be driven primarily by comprehensive carbon pricing and technology-neutral market mechanisms, with any justified public support being competitive, time-limited and focused on outcomes rather than the large-scale roll-out of a specific technology;

II\. Competitive markets and honest price signals

- carbon pricing to remain a central market-based instrument for reducing greenhouse gas emissions, in line with existing LYMEC policy, while energy taxation and electricity levies should increasingly reflect measurable externalities and cease systematically disadvantaging low-carbon electricity relative to the direct use of fossil fuels;
- the minimum excise rates on electricity under the Energy Taxation Directive to be reduced to zero;
- the progressive phase-out of environmentally harmful fossil-fuel subsidies and permanent operating subsidies for commercially mature generation technologies, with any justified public support for generation, capacity, storage or flexibility being transparent, proportionate, time-limited and competitively allocated wherever different resources can provide the required service;
- support schemes to preserve electricity-market signals, including incentives to reduce output when prices are negative or additional generation would aggravate congestion rather than rewarding production irrespective of its system value;
- broad wholesale or retail price caps and permanent administrative price-setting to be avoided, with affordability concerns addressed through targeted and transparent support; any exceptional emergency intervention should be temporary, targeted and designed to minimise distortions to price signals, competition, investment and cross-border trade;
- capacity mechanisms to be used only where transparent assessment demonstrates a remaining resource-adequacy problem, to be regularly reassessed, and to procure the required service rather than a predetermined technology, allowing non-discriminatory participation by demand response, storage, eligible cross-border capacity and firm low-carbon generation;
- deeper and more liquid European forward markets and fewer unnecessary barriers to bilateral and cross-border Power Purchase Agreements, alongside a broad choice of fixed-price, dynamic-price and long-term contracts so consumers and businesses can voluntarily choose how to allocate electricity-price risk;
- consumers, storage operators and other distributed resources to have non-discriminatory access to wholesale, balancing, congestion-management and ancillary-service markets, directly or through independent aggregators, with consumers free to monetise their flexibility without unnecessary dependence on their electricity supplier and without double counting;
- Member States to complete smart-meter deployment and ensure secure, standardised and user-controlled access to relevant consumption data for consumers and authorised service providers, enabling competition in dynamic tariffs, aggregation and other innovative electricity services;
- network tariffs to be transparent, non-discriminatory, technology-neutral and cost-reflective, allocating costs according to system use and cost causation while providing appropriate temporal and, where justified, locational signals for flexibility and congestion avoidance;
- electricity-market rules to permit competitive local flexibility and congestion-management markets where demand response, storage or generation can resolve constraints more efficiently than immediate network reinforcement;
- effective competitive neutrality between publicly and privately owned electricity companies, including equal access to markets and networks and consistent application of competition, procurement and State aid rules;
- stable and predictable frameworks for long-term electricity investment, avoiding retroactive changes to taxes, market rules or support schemes except where compelling public-interest considerations require them while preserving prospective democratic reform;
- assessments of competing technologies and public investment decisions to consider relevant full-system costs and benefits rather than relying solely on headline generation costs.

III\. A genuinely European and resilient electricity system

- the further completion of the European internal electricity market through sufficient cross-border interconnection, effective market coupling and the removal of unnecessary national regulatory barriers to electricity trade;
- European grid planning to reflect system-wide needs across the Union, including expected generation, demand, storage, electrification and cross-border flows, and to develop strategic high-capacity corridors, including HVDC infrastructure where justified by transparent system-needs and cost-benefit assessments;
- the costs of cross-border grid infrastructure to be allocated transparently according to measurable benefits and cost causation, preventing projects with substantial European benefits from being blocked because costs are concentrated in individual host or transit countries;
- transmission and distribution system operators to be able to undertake independently scrutinised anticipatory investments where credible scenarios demonstrate future capacity needs, while publishing sufficiently granular and regularly updated information on existing and planned connection capacity and network constraints;
- scarce grid-connection capacity to be allocated through transparent, non-discriminatory rules based on credible project readiness, objective milestones and the removal of inactive projects, while allowing voluntary flexible connection agreements that trade earlier access for clearly defined limitations on network use;
- permitting for generation, storage and grid infrastructure to become faster, fully digital and predictable through single points of contact, once-only data submission, transparent case tracking and clear deadlines, while authorities systematically remove duplicate or unnecessary procedures without weakening substantive environmental protection, public participation, property rights or safety standards;
- closer cooperation among European nuclear-safety authorities on standardised reactor technologies, including joint early reviews, reuse of technical assessments and common industrial and technical standards for Small and Advanced Modular Reactors where appropriate, while preserving the independence and final responsibility of competent national safety regulators;
- Europe to diversify strategic nuclear supply chains—including uranium sourcing, conversion, enrichment, fuel fabrication, critical components and specialised services—and maintain the scientific, engineering, industrial and regulatory expertise required to safely operate, assess, decommission and develop nuclear technologies;
- greater European cooperation on radioactive-waste management, including a framework for voluntary multinational geological repositories where these provide safer or more efficient solutions, subject to independent regulation, the polluter-pays principle, informed consent of the host state and affected communities, and continued financial responsibility of the state or operator generating the waste.
- Strengthened European cooperation to protect critical electricity infrastructure, including offshore installations, interconnectors, substations and digitally connected energy systems, against physical sabotage, cyberattacks and hybrid threats, through coordinated risk assessments, joint resilience exercises, secure supply chains and cross-border emergency response mechanisms;
