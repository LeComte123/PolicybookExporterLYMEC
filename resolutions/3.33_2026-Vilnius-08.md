Considering that:

- Cybersecurity threats, including increasingly AI-enabled attacks, are growing in sophistication and increasingly affect European democratic institutions, critical infrastructure and the economy, while growing reliance on digital systems makes cyber resilience essential to public safety, fundamental rights and European sovereignty;
- Fragmented implementation, overlapping reporting requirements and inconsistent supervision can undermine cybersecurity and the European Single Market;
- European cybersecurity companies face barriers to scaling, while public and private organisations need accessible, trustworthy and affordable security solutions;
- NIS2 transposition remains deeply uneven across Member States, with significant divergences in the definition of “significant incident”, in notification deadlines and in administrative procedures, creating a fragmented compliance burden for companies operating across multiple markets;
- Advances in artificial intelligence, autonomous systems and computing can strengthen cyber defence while changing attack capabilities and challenging existing safeguards, including cryptographic protection;
- Dependence on a single technology provider or jurisdiction can expose essential services to disruption, coercion and supply-chain compromise;

Noting that:

- European cybersecurity legislation, including NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) and the Cyber Solidarity Act, together with the AI Act, provides a basis for enforceable protection that must evolve with threats;
- A dedicated EU-wide certification scheme for Managed Security Services (MSS) is being developed under the Cybersecurity Act, but the market is not yet fully covered by the “certify once, sell anywhere” principle;
- Interoperability, data portability and the ability to switch providers can reduce strategic dependencies and improve resilience;
- Cooperation through ENISA, national incident response teams and CERT-EU provides a basis for shared exercises, threat information exchange and assistance across borders; and can be extended through working arrangements to trusted European non-EU partners such as Switzerland, whose economies, supply chains and critical infrastructure are closely interconnected with the Single Market.
- Ensuring proportionate oversight and incident reporting for high-risk AI systems used in cybersecurity and critical services, including appropriate human oversight and safeguards for autonomous systems;
- The debate over the EU Cloud Services Scheme (EUCS) has been stalled for years by the conflict between sovereignty requirements (EU headquarters, European control) and open-market principles, without a stable balance having yet been found;
- The transition to post-quantum cryptography constitutes a strategic resilience challenge that current EU cybersecurity policy has not fully addressed, despite the timeline already set by the Commission and the NIS Cooperation Group;
- European SMEs, particularly cybersecurity startups, report regulatory compliance burden, not lack of market opportunity, as their primary practical obstacle;
- The European Union is exploring light mutual-recognition arrangements on cybersecurity certification with trusted non-EU partners, including Japan, Singapore, Canada and the United Kingdom, allowing EU-certified products and services to gain streamlined access to their markets;
- The application of the first reporting obligations under the CRA from September 2026 marks a turning point for product security, but risks adding a dual layer of incident reporting alongside NIS2 for hardware and software manufacturers;

Believing that:

- Cybersecurity is a shared responsibility, requiring clear and proportionate duties for public authorities, technology providers and operators;
- Security requirements should reflect the likelihood and severity of harm and the criticality of services, with practical support for smaller organisations and public oversight proportionate to risk;
- Technology-neutral rules should protect innovation, competition and legitimate research, while requiring evidence of adequate safeguards for serious risks; building European capabilities should reduce strategic dependencies while preserving secure international cooperation;
- Strategic autonomy requires skills, secure infrastructure and viable alternatives, alongside cooperation with democratic partners and evidence-based assessment of foreign interference risks;
- European strategic autonomy in encryption and critical infrastructure must rest on clear, funded technical timelines, not only on general principles of dependency reduction;
- Cybersecurity must uphold privacy, strong encryption, due process and democratic accountability; technical standards must remain adaptable without weakening these principles;
- Mutual recognition of cybersecurity certification with trusted non-EU partners should be pursued as a complement to, not a substitute for, a strong and harmonised internal European framework;

LYMEC calls for:

1\. Strengthening the European cybersecurity workforce by:

- Promoting common European cybersecurity skills frameworks and qualifications, while recognising practical experience and diverse entry routes;
- Expanding partnerships between education providers, public authorities and businesses for apprenticeships, traineeships and lifelong learning, including recruitment pathways for local government and underrepresented groups;
- Embedding practical digital security education in schools and regular workforce training, including for decision-makers, adapted to evolving fraud, social engineering and incident-response needs;
- Providing accessible funding and technical assistance to SMEs and municipalities for cybersecurity assessments, remediation and shared expertise;
- Supporting the CSC-EDIC in implementing the ECSF and encouraging broader Member State participation;

2\. Strengthening the European cybersecurity Single Market by:

- Calling on EU institutions and Member States to ensure coherent implementation, effective supervision and interoperable incident reporting, while regularly reviewing requirements to keep them proportionate and effective;
- Promoting interoperable standards and proportionate certification and procurement requirements, ensuring that certification is carried out by independent conformity assessment bodies accredited by national accreditation bodies and supervised by national cybersecurity certification authorities under the Cybersecurity Act, and that valid certificates, including ISO/IEC 27001 certificates covering the relevant requirements, are recognised across Member States, while avoiding duplicate certification;
- Harmonising the definition of “significant incident” and reporting timelines across Member States as a priority within the ongoing NIS2 review, to reduce the fragmented compliance landscape currently faced by cross-border entities;
- Ensuring that manufacturers placing digital products on the EU market provide security by design and default, clearly disclose the applicable security support period before purchase and provide necessary security fixes during that period without separate charges, consistently with the Cyber Resilience Act;
- Promoting clear end-of-support notices and practical migration guidance so that users can make informed choices about replacing unsupported products;
- Establishing clear legal safeguards for good-faith security researchers who minimise harm, protect personal data and follow coordinated vulnerability disclosure procedures;
- Accelerating the finalisation of EUCS through a risk-based approach that balances security, strategic autonomy and competition;
- Completing an EU-wide MSS certification scheme that enables cybersecurity service providers, including SMEs, to benefit from the “certify once, sell anywhere” principle;
- Addressing AI-related cybersecurity risks through existing regulatory frameworks, with any additional independent testing limited to clearly defined capabilities or uses presenting demonstrable risks of serious harm; recognising equivalent assessments, protecting confidential information and personal data in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), and avoiding duplicate approval procedures;
- Requiring operators deploying autonomous systems in critical services to limit access to necessary functions, maintain appropriate activity records and test human intervention and service recovery arrangements, proportionate to the consequences of failure; integrating serious-incident reporting into existing notification channels;
- Ensuring that any authority-imposed remediation requirement or deployment restriction addresses a documented risk, is no broader or longer than necessary, and is subject to reasoned decisions, periodic review and effective appeal;
- Reducing compliance burden for SMEs and startups through simplified reporting templates, a genuine one-stop shop for incident notification, and proportionate documentation requirements, in line with the Commission’s Digital Omnibus simplification package, proposed in late 2025 and currently under legislative debate;

3\. Strengthening European strategic resilience without protectionism by:

- Calling on Member States and EU institutions to secure public services, including municipal services, through funded, risk-based requirements covering access control, updates, protected backups and tested continuity plans; requiring independent audits at risk-appropriate intervals, assigned remediation deadlines and supervisory follow-up, while testing essential service provision during digital outages;
- Exploring a common European model agreement on ‘data embassies’, meaning the hosting of backup copies of critical government data and essential public-service systems of one Member State on the territory of another under a treaty-based regime of inviolability and immunity, which would: cover government data only and define the permitted purpose of the facility; exclude remote access by the host State without consent; keep the facility under the sending State’s control rather than a provider subject to third-country jurisdiction; apply recognised security and data-centre standards; provide for a joint committee, dispute resolution and orderly termination; and comply with the GDPR and NIS2.
- Strengthening cooperation between national incident response teams, ENISA and CERT-EU, including mutual assistance and exercises involving municipalities and essential-service providers; drawing on the expertise of the NATO Cooperative Cyber Defense Centre of Excellence (CCDCOE) in Tallinn, including tis Locked Shields exercise and the Tallinn Manual on international law applicable to cyber operations.
- Strengthening and adequately funding the EU Cybersecurity Reserve beyond its current scope and budget, so it can respond to a broader range of incidents and support Member States with limited national capacity;
- Calling on public administrations and critical infrastructure operators to inventory vulnerable cryptographic systems, prioritise data requiring long-term confidentiality, and establish phased migration plans with identified responsibilities, costs and supplier requirements, aligned with the European post-quantum roadmap;
- Supporting implementation through shared technical guidance, interoperability testing and pooled procurement; using existing funding programmes for targeted, time-limited assistance where SMEs and public administrations demonstrate capacity gaps, with progress measured against defined migration milestones;
- Requiring critical infrastructure operators to manage supplier concentration and supply-chain risks and maintain viable continuity and exit arrangements;
- Supporting European cybersecurity startups, SMEs and open-source security projects through access to research, finance and procurement;
- Exploring links between European cybersecurity industrial policy and the emerging European Defence Single Market to facilitate appropriate access to dual-use funding and cooperation;
- Deepening cooperation with democratic partners through joint cyber exercises, operational exchanges between incident response teams and cooperation on interoperable security standards;
- Opposing mandated encryption backdoors that weaken end-to-end encryption and indiscriminate scanning of private communications, while supporting targeted, judicially authorised and proportionate investigative measures consistent with Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union;
- Limiting threat information sharing to necessary, proportionate purposes, with data minimisation, retention limits and oversight by existing competent authorities;
- Pursuing mutual-recognition arrangements on cybersecurity certification with trusted non-EU partners such as Japan, Singapore, Canada, Ukraine, Switzerland and the United Kingdom, to open new export opportunities for European cybersecurity companies without compromising EU security and data protections standards

Strengthening Europe’s capacity to independently develop, evaluate and operate capable AI models and secure computing infrastructure, reducing strategic dependencies on providers subject to US or Chinese jurisdiction, by:

- securing access to sufficient high-performance computing, secure cloud infrastructure and competitive research funding for European universities, public research institutions, startups and SMEs;
- supporting European AI research and development, including open-source models and tools, through transparent, competitive funding focused on research, shared infrastructure and demonstrable capability gaps;
- ensuring that public procurement promotes interoperability, data portability and the practical ability to switch providers or operate models independently, with safeguards proportionate to the sensitivity and criticality of their use;
- diversifying critical hardware and infrastructure supply chains while preserving competition and scientific cooperation with trusted democratic partners.

4\. Strengthening European operational crisis preparedness by:

- Expanding coordinated cross-border cyber crisis exercises building on existing EU exercise frameworks and the EU Cybersecurity Reserve;
- Supporting Member States with limited national CSIRT capacity through shared resources and mutual assistance mechanisms;
- Integrating post-quantum cryptography readiness into critical infrastructure resilience assessments;
